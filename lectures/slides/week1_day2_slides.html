What Is Reproducibility?

Someone else — or future you — can take your data and your process and arrive at the same results.

Not just the same numbers.

The same reasoning.

Three Types of Reproducibility

Computational: Your code runs the same way on different computers

Empirical: Your data collection is documented well enough to repeat

Conceptual: Your decisions are clear enough to understand why

Today’s focus: Conceptual reproducibility

Why This Matters

You’re always collaborating with future-you.

And past-you doesn’t respond to emails.

If you don’t write down why you made a decision:

- You won’t remember it in two weeks

- You can’t defend it

- You can’t reproduce it

Tool: The Data Decision Log

A running document where you write:

  • What you decided
  • Why you decided it
  • What else you considered
  • What trade-offs you made

Google Design Docs - How engineers document decisions: https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/

Google Documentation Best Practices: https://google.github.io/styleguide/docguide/best_practices.html

Example Decision Log Entry

Decision: Converted all “dawn” entries to 6:00 AM.

Reasoning: Dawn in our study area ranges from 5:45–6:15 AM. Using 6:00 introduces <30 minutes of error, acceptable given our 2-hour observation windows.

Alternative considered: Exclude “dawn” entries entirely. Rejected because it removes 18% of early-season observations.

Trade-off: Slight temporal imprecision in exchange for retaining sample size.

What This Entry Does

  • Transparent — not hiding reasoning

  • Shows alternatives — not just first idea

  • Acknowledges trade-offs — doesn’t pretend the decision was obvious

This is scientific thinking applied to data work.

The Scenario

You’re analyzing bee diversity data from urban gardens. Your dataset has a “Garden Type” column with:

  • Community garden
  • Community Garden
  • community garden
  • Comm. garden
  • School garden
  • school garden (community-maintained)
  • Private yard
  • private yard (open to public)

Task: Write a decision log entry explaining how you would standardize these categories — and why.

Silent Writing Time

10 minutes

Start now.

Peer Review

Breakout rooms of 3 (12 minutes):

  1. Each person reads their entry aloud (1-2 min)

  2. Others give feedback:

    • One thing that was clear
    • One place you wanted more explanation

Goal: Help each other see where clarity breaks down

What Made Writing Hard?

Not the specifics of your scenarios—

What made clarity hard?

What made writing difficult?

(Chat or unmute)

“How much detail should I include?”

Guideline:

Write enough that a colleague could understand your reasoning without asking follow-up questions.

If they’d need you there to explain it, you didn’t write enough.

“Is my reasoning good enough?”

There’s no “good enough” in the abstract.

There’s reasoning that’s defensible given your constraints and reasoning that’s not defensible.

Not defensible: “I combined them because they seemed similar”

Defensible: “I combined community and school gardens into ‘public gardens’ because both involve shared maintenance and open access, even though governance structures differ”

“I hadn’t thought through alternatives”

That’s the value of writing it down.

Writing reveals what you haven’t thought about yet.

That’s not a bug — that’s a feature.

“It was hard to admit trade-offs”

We’re trained to present conclusions, not uncertainty.

But science IS trade-offs.

Pretending they don’t exist doesn’t make your work stronger—

It makes it fragile.

The Core Pattern

Writing forces you to be explicit about things you thought you’d already decided.

That’s uncomfortable.

That’s essential.

When Do You Use This?

Write in your decision log when:

  1. Choosing between two reasonable options
  2. Combining or recoding categories
  3. Excluding data (ALWAYS)
  4. Making an assumption that could be challenged
  5. Something didn’t work (so future-you doesn’t repeat it)

The Test

If someone asked in a meeting:

“Why did you do it that way?”

And your honest answer is:

“Um… I’m not sure”

→ You should have written it down.

How This Connects to Structure

Decision logs record your reasoning.

But they only work if your files and workflow are also reproducible.

Organized project:

- One folder per project (RProject)

- Organized subfolders (raw data, processed data, scripts, figures)

- Relative file paths (works on anyone’s computer)

Why Structure Matters

If I can’t find your data

and I can’t run your code,

your decision log is just a story.

It’s not reproducible.

What We’re Building Toward

A way of working where:

  • Your data stays raw (never overwrite originals)
  • Your process is scripted (no manual copy-paste)
  • Your decisions are documented
  • Your file paths are robust (work on any computer)

Everything connects.
