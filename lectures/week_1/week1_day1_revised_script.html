Week 1, Day 1: Data Science as Scientific Practice

Biology/Chemistry 806

1 Week 1, Day 1: Data Science as Scientific Practice

Duration: 75 minutes | Online Synchronous | 30 students

1.1 Pre-Class Setup (Instructor)

  • Test Zoom/conferencing platform
  • Prepare Google Doc template for breakout activity
  • Queue up welcome slide
  • Have chat window visible
  • Prepare breakout rooms (groups of 3-4, total 8-10 rooms)

1.2 SLIDE 1: Welcome

[0:00–0:05] — Arrival and Settling

Good morning, everyone! Welcome to Biology/Chemistry 806. I’m Gretchen LeBuhn, and I’m co-teaching this course with Nicole Adelstein. We’re really glad you’re here.

Before we jump in, let’s take a moment to get settled. Grab water or tea if you need it, adjust your audio settings, and find a comfortable spot. We’re going to be together for 75 minutes, and I want you to feel present and ready to engage.

[Pause 3-4 seconds]

Quick tech check: If you can hear me clearly, give me a thumbs-up reaction or type “yes” in the chat.

Great! If your audio cuts out or something breaks during class, just pop a message in the chat and we’ll sort it out.

1.3 SLIDE 2: What This Course Is Really About

[0:05–0:10] — Setting the Tone

Before we dive into activities, I want to be clear about what this course is and what it isn’t.

This is a course about thinking clearly with data.

It’s not about being a coder already. It’s not about memorizing functions or learning statistics in isolation. It’s about learning to explain your reasoning, spot your own assumptions, and revise when something doesn’t make sense.

If you can do that kind of thinking in biology or chemistry, you can do it with data in R. If you struggle to do it in R, that’s not a coding problem — it’s a thinking problem, and we’ll work on it together.

[Pause]

Some of you might be wondering if you belong here. Maybe you’ve never written a line of code. Maybe you’ve used R a little and felt completely lost.

Let me be very clear: prior coding experience is not what matters here.

What matters is whether you’re willing to ask “why,” explain your choices, and revise when things are unclear. That’s science. We’re just applying it to data work.

1.4 SLIDE 3: How Today Will Work

[0:10–0:13] — Structure Preview

Here’s what we’re doing today:

  1. Get to know each other — not in a forced icebreaker way, but in a way that helps us start noticing patterns in data
  2. Use that process to surface big data science ideas — without touching code yet
  3. Talk about norms — what you want from this class, what I want, and how we’ll work together

I’m not testing you today. I’m watching how you think, not what you already know.

Any questions before we start? You can unmute or use the chat.

1.5 SLIDE 4: Introductions as Data

[0:13–0:16] — Setting Up the Chat Activity

Okay, first activity. I want everyone to put three things in the chat:

  1. Your name
  2. Your field or program
  3. One kind of data you work with — or hope to work with

If you’re not sure what counts as “data,” that’s totally fine. Just write what comes to mind. If you’ve never worked with data, write that. All answers are useful here.

You’ve got about two minutes. Go ahead and start typing!

[0:16–0:19] — Silent Typing Time

[Monitor chat. If people seem stuck after 30 seconds, you can add: “I’m seeing some great responses coming in. Remember, there’s no wrong answer here.”]

[0:19–0:26] — Reading and Commentary

Okay, I’m going to read some of these out loud — not to put anyone on the spot, but because I want us to start noticing patterns together.

[Read 6-8 responses, pausing to make observations]:

  • “Excel spreadsheets” — Notice that’s a format, not the phenomenon itself. Interesting distinction.
  • “Survey responses about student stress” — That’s behavioral data with a specific context.
  • “I’m not sure” — That’s an important answer too. Uncertainty is data about where we are in our learning.
  • “Gene sequences” — Molecular data with a very specific structure.

[Pause]

Let’s pause here. We just asked one simple question, and we got very different kinds of answers.

Here’s my question for you — and you can answer in the chat or unmute:

Did everyone interpret “data” the same way?

[0:26–0:30] — Processing Responses

[Read 3-4 chat responses aloud]

Yes. Exactly. Some people answered with the format (Excel, CSV). Some answered with the content (genes, behavior). Some answered with the collection method (surveys, sensors).

And here’s the thing: that ambiguity doesn’t go away when you start coding. It just gets buried under syntax.

In this class, we’re going to keep surfacing it. Because the hardest part of data science isn’t learning functions — it’s deciding what you’re even measuring and how to define it clearly.

1.6 SLIDE 5: Turning This Class Into a Dataset

[0:30–0:33] — Setting Up Breakout Activity

Okay, next activity. I’m going to put you into small groups of 3-4 people for about 10 minutes.

Here’s your task: Imagine you need to describe this class as a dataset for someone who wasn’t here today.

  • What variables would you include?
  • How would you define them?
  • What information do you wish you had?

I don’t want just a list of variable names. I want your reasoning. Why did you choose those variables? Where did you have to make judgment calls?

You’re going to work in a shared Google Doc — I’m dropping the template link in the chat right now.

[Drop link in chat]

Questions before I send you out?

[0:33–0:35] — Clarifications

[Answer 1-2 questions if needed]

Great. I’m sending you to breakout rooms now. Take the full 10 minutes — this is not a race.

I’ll pop into rooms briefly to check in. See you back here at [state exact time, e.g., 12:48].

[0:35–0:45] — BREAKOUT ROOMS

[Instructor visits 2-3 rooms briefly, listens, maybe asks one clarifying question]

[0:45–0:47] — Transition Back

Welcome back! I know 10 minutes goes fast when you’re actually thinking.

I’m going to ask three groups to share what they came up with — not everything, just one interesting decision you made or one place where you got stuck.

Can I have a volunteer from Group 3 to start us off? You can unmute or type in chat.

1.7 SLIDE 6: What Did We Learn?

[0:47–0:58] — Report-Outs and Reframing

[Call on three groups. After each shares for ~2 minutes, you reframe]:

Example Group 1 shares about struggling to define “experience”:

I want to repeat that, because it’s important.

You wanted to measure “experience,” but you realized it’s not one thing. It could be time in a program, it could be prior statistics courses, it could be comfort with ambiguity, it could be years working with data.

And here’s what matters: You didn’t fail because you couldn’t agree. You succeeded because you noticed the decision you’d have to make — and that decision has consequences.

If you measure experience as “years in grad school,” you’ll get a different analysis than if you measure it as “number of R courses taken.” Both are defensible. Neither is invisible.

That’s data science.

[Repeat this pattern for 2 more groups, ~3-4 minutes each]

[0:58–1:02] — Explicit Connection

Okay, let’s step back. What did we just do?

Without writing a single line of code, you: - Defined variables - Noticed ambiguity - Made decisions and justified them - Realized that someone else might make different decisions — and that’s okay

That’s the foundation of everything we’ll do this semester.

On Wednesday, we’ll start putting those decisions into words more formally using something called a “decision log.” But today, I wanted you to feel what it’s like to think carefully about data before you touch a computer.

1.8 SLIDE 7: Course Norms

[1:02–1:05] — Transition

We’ve got about 13 minutes left, and I want to spend them talking about how we’ll work together this semester.

I’m going to share a few norms I think are important. Then I want to hear from you — what do you need from this class? What do you want to accomplish?

[1:05–1:10] — Your Commitments

Here’s what I’m committing to:

  1. I will not assume you know things. If I use a term you haven’t seen, stop me.

  2. I will not mistake speed for understanding. Going slow is not a problem. Going fast without learning is.

  3. I will not treat “I don’t know” as failure. It’s the start of figuring something out.

  4. I will give you time to think before you answer. Silence is not dead air — it’s thinking space.

And here’s what I need from you:

  1. Ask questions out loud, not just in your head. If you’re confused, someone else is too.

  2. Try things even when you’re not sure. I’d rather see your messy reasoning than a polished guess.

  3. Be kind to each other and to yourselves. This course is hard because thinking clearly is hard, not because I’m trying to trick you.

[1:10–1:12] — Opening It Up

Okay, your turn. I’m going to ask two questions. You can answer in the chat or unmute:

  1. What do you want to accomplish this semester?
  2. What do you need from me or from each other to make that happen?

Take a moment to think. Then start sharing.

[Give 5-7 seconds of silence]

[1:12–1:18] — Student Voices

[Read responses, acknowledge them briefly]:

“I want to feel competent, not just compliant.” — Yes. That’s exactly what we’re here for.

“I want to understand what I’m doing, not just copy code.” — Absolutely. Understanding is the goal.

“I need patience when I’m stuck.” — You’ll have it. From me and from each other.

[1:18–1:20] — Synthesis

I’m hearing a few themes: - You want to feel competent, not just compliant - You want to understand, not just copy - You want space to struggle without judgment

I can work with that. We all can. Let’s hold each other to these commitments.

1.9 SLIDE 8: Before Wednesday

[1:20–1:22] — Preview and Closing

Before Wednesday, I want you to think about this question:

What decisions did we make today that felt invisible at the time?

We’re not writing code yet, but we’re already making choices about what counts, what matters, and how to define things.

On Wednesday, we’ll practice writing those choices down clearly using something called a decision log. It’s going to feel weird at first. That’s okay.

[1:22–1:25] — Final Thoughts

One last thing before we go.

Everything we did today — the thinking, the struggling with definitions, the noticing ambiguity — that’s not “pre-data-science.”

That IS data science.

The coding is just execution. The thinking is the work.

See you Wednesday. Thanks for being here and for engaging so thoughtfully.

[Stay online for 2-3 minutes for questions]

1.11 Instructor Notes

  • Pacing: Build in pauses. Online students need processing time.
  • Engagement: Call on specific groups/people by name when possible
  • Backup plan: If breakout rooms fail, use collaborative Google Doc with sections for each “group”
  • Accessibility: Keep chat open for those who prefer not to unmute
