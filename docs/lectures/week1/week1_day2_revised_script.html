SLIDE 3: Let’s Talk About Reproducibility

[0:10–0:15] — The Problem

Let me show you something.

[Show slide: messy Excel workflow — copy/paste chaos, files named “Final_v2_REAL_final.xlsx”]

Does this look familiar?

You work in Excel, you calculate something, you copy it into Word, you make a figure, you paste that too. Maybe you email a version to yourself. Maybe you save three copies with slightly different names.

Then your advisor says: “Can you redo this with the updated data?”

And you think: “…I have no idea what I did.”

[Pause for effect]

Sound familiar? You can admit it. We’ve all been there.

[0:15–0:19] — Defining Reproducibility

Reproducibility means: Someone else — or future you — can take your data and your process and arrive at the same results.

Not just the same numbers. The same reasoning.

There are different kinds of reproducibility:

  • Computational: Your code runs the same way on different computers
  • Empirical: Your data collection is documented well enough that someone could repeat it
  • Conceptual: Your decisions are clear enough that someone understands why you did what you did

We’re focusing on that last one today — conceptual reproducibility.

[0:19–0:22] — Why This Matters

Here’s the thing: You’re always collaborating with future-you.

And past-you doesn’t respond to emails.

If you don’t write down why you made a decision, you won’t remember it in two weeks. You definitely won’t remember it in two months. And if you can’t remember it, you can’t defend it.

SLIDE 4: A Tool — The Data Decision Log

[0:22–0:30] — Introducing the Log

So here’s a tool I want you to use: a data decision log.

It’s just a running document where you write down: - What you decided - Why you decided it - What else you considered - What trade-offs you made

Let me show you an example.

[Show slide with example entry]:

Decision: Converted all "dawn" entries to 6:00 AM.

Reasoning: Dawn in our study area ranges from 5:45–6:15 AM during 
our sampling period. Using 6:00 introduces less than 30 minutes of 
error, which is acceptable given our 2-hour observation windows.

Alternative considered: Exclude "dawn" entries entirely. Rejected 
because it removes 18% of early-season observations, which would 
bias our temporal analysis toward mid-day.

Trade-off: Slight temporal imprecision in exchange for retaining 
sample size and avoiding seasonal bias.

[0:30–0:32] — What This Does

Notice what this entry does:

  • It’s transparent — you’re not hiding your reasoning
  • It shows you thought about alternatives — not just the first thing that came to mind
  • It acknowledges trade-offs instead of pretending the decision was obvious

This is scientific thinking applied to data work.

Any questions about what a decision log is or why we’d use one? Chat or unmute.

[1:05–1:08] — Transition Back

[1:08–1:17] — Harvesting Insights

[Read and respond to 4-5 student observations]:

“I didn’t know how much detail to include.”

Yes. Here’s my guideline: Write enough that a colleague could understand your reasoning without asking follow-up questions. If they’d need you there to explain it, you didn’t write enough.

“I wasn’t sure if my reasoning was good enough.”

Let me reframe that. There’s no such thing as reasoning that’s “good enough” in the abstract. There’s reasoning that’s defensible given your constraints — and reasoning that’s not defensible.

“I combined them because they seemed similar” → not defensible

“I combined community and school gardens into ‘public gardens’ because both involve shared maintenance structures and open access, even though governance differs” → defensible

See the difference?

“I realized I hadn’t thought through alternatives.”

That’s the value of writing it down. Writing reveals what you haven’t thought about yet. That’s not a bug — that’s a feature.

“It was hard to admit trade-offs.”

Yes. Because we’re trained to present conclusions, not uncertainty. But science is trade-offs. Every decision has costs. Pretending they don’t exist doesn’t make your work stronger — it makes it fragile.

[1:17–1:19] — Naming the Core Pattern

Here’s what I’m hearing:

Writing forces you to be explicit about things you thought you’d already decided.

That’s uncomfortable. It’s also essential.

Because when you start coding — which we will soon — you’ll make these same decisions inside filter commands and mutate operations. And if you haven’t written them down, you won’t remember them two weeks later.

Or worse: you won’t be able to defend your own work.

SLIDE 9: When Do You Actually Use This?

[1:19–1:24] — Making It Practical

Practical question: When do you write in a decision log?

You don’t write every single decision. That would be paralyzing.

You write when:

  1. You’re choosing between two reasonable options (not when the answer is obvious)
  2. You’re combining or recoding categories
  3. You’re excluding data — ALWAYS document exclusions
  4. You’re making an assumption that could be challenged
  5. You tried something and it didn’t work — so future-you doesn’t repeat the mistake

Here’s the test: If someone asked you in a meeting, “Why did you do it that way?” and your honest answer is “Um… I’m not sure,” that’s when you should have written it down.

SLIDE 10: How This Connects to Structure

[1:24–1:32] — Bridging to Workflows

Okay. Decision logs are about recording your reasoning. But they only work if your files and workflow are also reproducible.

Let me show you what I mean.

[Show slide: organized project structure with folders]

This is what a reproducible project looks like:

  • One folder per project (we’ll use RProjects)
  • Organized subfolders: raw data, processed data, scripts, figures
  • Relative file paths so your code works on anyone’s computer

Why does this matter?

Because if I can’t find your data, and I can’t run your code, your decision log is just a story. It’s not reproducible.

[1:32–1:37] — The Workflow Promise

Here’s what we’re building toward this semester:

A way of working where:

  • Your data stays raw (you never overwrite the original)
  • Your process is scripted (no manual copy-paste)
  • Your decisions are documented
  • Your file paths are robust (they work on my computer, your computer, future-you’s computer)

Today you practiced the decision log. Next week, we’ll build the structure. By Week 3, we’ll start writing actual code inside that structure.

Everything connects.
